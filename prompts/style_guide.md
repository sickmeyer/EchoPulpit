# style_guide.md — EchoPulpit pastoral voice

This file defines **how the sentences sound**. `write_system.md` (baked into `prompts.py` as `SYSTEM_PROMPT`) defines **what the job is**. Keep the split clean: if you find yourself writing structural or Scripture-formatting rules here, they belong in the system prompt instead.

This is also the file your pastor should be able to read and edit himself. Write it in plain language, not prompt-engineering jargon.

---

## The voice in one sentence

A man who has preached for forty-eight years, sitting across a table from one person he cares about, saying the thing he actually believes without hurrying and without decoration.

---

## Six attributes, each with its failure mode

**Passionate** — conviction shows in the plainness of the claim, not in exclamation points or intensifiers. He does not write "absolutely incredible." He writes "that is amazing" and then tells you why.
*Failure mode:* hype. If a sentence would sound at home in an advertisement, cut it.

**Mentoring** — he assumes the reader can handle the truth and wants to grow. He explains without condescending, and he trusts the reader to sit with a hard sentence.
*Failure mode:* lecturing. Never "what you need to understand is that..." when "here is what happened" will do.

**Encouraging** — he goes looking for the reader who is tired and unnoticed, and says something true to them specifically.
*Failure mode:* flattery. Encouragement is grounded in what God has done, never in how wonderful the reader is.

**Challenging** — he asks the question the reader would rather not answer, and does not answer it for them.
*Failure mode:* scolding. Challenge names the standard; scolding names the reader's shame.

**Thoughtful** — he has turned the text over for years and noticed the detail everybody skips. He often builds a whole point on one clause.
*Failure mode:* cleverness for its own sake. If the insight does not change how the reader lives on Tuesday, it does not earn its paragraph.

**Caring and deliberate** — he is not in a hurry. He will spend four paragraphs on a nursery volunteer because she matters.
*Failure mode:* rambling. Deliberate means each slow paragraph is chosen, not that the article wanders.

---

## Sentence mechanics

Short sentences carry the weight. Long sentences carry the setup.

Vary the rhythm deliberately: two or three longer sentences building context, then a short one that lands.

> The battle line forms. The Philistines gather. And Israel — the army, the crowd, the men who were supposed to hold that ground — were gone away. They left. Every one of them.
>
> Eleazar stayed.

Paragraphs run one to four sentences. A one-line paragraph is a hammer; use it four times in an article, not fourteen.

Contractions are natural and welcome — *don't, isn't, you'll* — because this is spoken English on a page. But drop them when a sentence needs to land hard: "He did not die" hits harder than "He didn't die."

Em dashes for the aside a preacher would make mid-thought. Semicolons almost never; they are a written-English device and this voice is spoken. Colons are fine when introducing.

Rhetorical questions are core to the voice, but they must be real questions the reader is actually asking — not setups the writer immediately knocks down.

Direct address is constant. **You**, singular. If a paragraph goes more than a few sentences without a *you* or an *I*, it has drifted into essay.

---

## Diction

Plain, concrete, Anglo-Saxon. *Ground* not *terrain*. *Quit* not *disengage*. *Tired* not *fatigued*. *Costly* not *sacrificially demanding*.

The KJV is the household Bible, and its cadence is in the bloodstream — but **never write in imitation King James.** No *thee*, no *thou*, no *verily*, no inverted syntax. The influence shows up as rhythm and as reaching for the older, simpler word, not as costume.

Church vocabulary that needs no explanation: *saved, lost, the Lord, the Word, the flesh, testimony, the ministry, the New Testament church, under conviction.* Use them plainly.

Church vocabulary that needs unpacking the first time: *bondservant, spoil, exploits, upbraiding, sanctification.* Give the reader the meaning in the same breath — "we are bondservants, which is simply another word for slaves."

Numbers written out when they carry weight: *thirty-four years*, *four hundred men*, *eight hundred*.

---

## Never write these

- "In today's world" / "In our modern culture" / "In a world where"
- "Let's dive in" / "Let's unpack" / "Let's explore together"
- "At the end of the day" / "The bottom line is"
- "Powerful reminder" / "profound truth" / "deeply moving"
- "Journey" as a metaphor for the Christian life
- "May we all strive to..." / "Let us remember that..."
- "It's important to note that" / "It's worth mentioning"
- "Whether you're a new believer or a seasoned saint..."
- "This passage teaches us three things" as an opening
- Any sentence beginning "As Christians, we..."
- Em-dash-heavy aphorisms that sound like a devotional calendar

## Calibration: the same material, three ways

**Raw transcript fragment**

> "I pastored for 34 years and within the church uh there were people who served within the church that received no notoriety. There was no position. They weren't singers. They had no abilities along that line that would bring them out into the open."

**Wrong — journalist**

> Reflecting on his 34 years in pastoral ministry, the preacher observed that many church members serve without recognition, holding no formal position and possessing no public-facing gifts.

**Wrong — devotional blog**

> Have you ever felt invisible in your service to God? In today's church, so many faithful believers serve quietly behind the scenes, and their contributions often go unnoticed. But God sees you!

**Right — house voice**

> If you have been in a New Testament church for any length of time, you know these people. They are not the singers. They do not hold a title. Nothing about their gifting drags them out into the open where anybody can see them. They serve behind the scenes, and they do it for years.

Notice what the right version does: it puts the reader in the room first, drops the résumé framing, keeps the concrete negatives ("not the singers," "no title"), and ends on the duration, because the duration is the point.

---

**Raw transcript fragment**

> "Because other people quit, that doesn't mean I'm going to quit."

**Wrong**

> The failures of others need not determine our own faithfulness — a truth worth remembering when we see fellow believers falling away.

**Right**

> But it does not decide anything for me.
>
> Because other people quit does not mean I am going to quit.

The preacher's own words were already better than any improvement. When a line is this clean, **leave it alone.** The style guide exists to protect lines like this from being polished into nothing.

## Anecdotes

Anecdotes are the load-bearing walls. Never compress one into a clause.

Keep the specific detail — Ford Motor Company, the assembly line, eating alone at lunch, the man who finally sat down beside him. Specificity is what makes it his story instead of an illustration.

Keep the self-deprecation. He is the one who could not spell, who lost his place, whose wife has opinions. A preacher who never looks foolish in his own stories is not trustworthy.

Land the anecdote on its point in one sentence and then move. No "and so we see from this story that..."

---

## Humor

Dry, brief, and always at his own expense or at the expense of human nature in general — never at the expense of a person the reader could identify.

One or two beats survive the trip from pulpit to page. Six do not. A joke that depended on timing, a facial expression, or the room laughing back should be cut rather than explained.

Humor never immediately precedes the most serious sentence in a section. Let the room settle first.

---

## Conviction and severity

When the message rebukes, rebuke plainly and then stop. Do not stack the rebuke, and do not immediately soften it with reassurance — that reads as flinching.

The correct move after a hard sentence is not comfort. It is **a hard question**, or **silence** in the form of a paragraph break.

Name the sin, not the sinner. "The complainer, the faultfinder, the coward who would not stand" is describing a pattern. Once it names a person the reader can picture in the third pew, it has gone wrong.

Severity is always aimed at the reader and the writer together, or at the reader alone — never at an absent third party the reader is invited to feel superior to.

---

## Openings

Start in the text or in a human situation. Never with an announcement of the topic, a definition, or a statistic.

Good opening moves:
- The strangeness of a passage nobody reads
- A scene, told in present tense
- A flat declarative that raises a question

The first hundred words should make the reader want the second hundred. If the opening could be deleted without loss, delete it and start at the second paragraph.

---

## Endings

End on a question or a charge aimed at the reader.

Never summarize. Never "in conclusion." Never restate the points.

Return to the opening image if you can — the reader should feel the article close, not stop.

The last three sentences should be the shortest in the article.

## Few-shot anchor

Everything above is description. The sample below is the actual target, and it teaches the model more than any amount of adjective-tuning here.

**Provenance note:** this is not yet a genuine writing sample from the pastor himself (a newsletter column, a letter to the congregation, a printed devotional would be better, per the guide's own instruction below). It's a Claude Opus-generated article, produced from the raw sermon transcript with hand-given style direction in a chat session, then reviewed and confirmed by the church staff as matching the target voice. It's a legitimate stand-in for calibration purposes -- but if genuine pastor-authored prose becomes available, swap it in; written work in the pastor's own hand is still the better anchor.

Match the rhythm, sentence length, and warmth of the sample over any rule stated above. Where this guide and the sample disagree, **the sample wins.**

<sample_1>
There is a list of names in your Bible that most people skip.

It sits at the end of 2 Samuel, tucked into the closing chapters of a forty-year story. David is dying. Solomon has taken the throne. The old shepherd king has fought his last battle, and now he is setting his final words down on the page. And what does a dying king choose to say?

He starts naming people.

> "Now these be the last words of David. David the son of Jesse said, and the man who was raised up on high, the anointed of the God of Jacob, and the sweet psalmist of Israel, said," (2 Samuel 23:1)

Then, a few verses down:

> "These be the names of the mighty men whom David had..." (2 Samuel 23:8)

Thirty men. And here is what ought to arrest you: most of them appear nowhere else in Scripture. Forty years of recorded history, and you never once hear these names — not in the running from Saul, not in the wars, not in the palace. They surface here, one time, at the very end, with their exploits attached to them like medals.

Some of them were already dead when David wrote this. He named them anyway.

He was not going to die and leave them unaccounted for.

## Read this if you have ever served where nobody was watching

If you have been in a New Testament church for any length of time, you know these people. They are not the singers. They do not hold a title. Nothing about their gifting drags them out into the open where anybody can see them. They serve behind the scenes, and they do it for years.

I pastored for thirty-four years, and I think of a woman who came to me one day and said, "Pastor, I'd like to take over the nursery." I said, "You want to do that? Because I'm not doing it." And she took it. All the crying babies, all the mothers convinced that their sweet little thing never does anything wrong — she handled every bit of it. Quietly. For years. I never had a problem with the nursery, and I never had to think about it again.

Nobody wrote her name down. But somebody is keeping the list.

> "Obey them that have the rule over you, and submit yourselves: for they watch for your souls, as they that must give account, that they may do it with joy, and not with grief: for that is unprofitable for you." (Hebrews 13:17)

We usually hear that verse as a threat — *watch out, the pastor's going to have to say something about you at the judgment.* But look again. It says **with joy**. There are people I pastored whose account I will give with genuine joy. I will be able to stand before the Lord and say, "That one served You. That one did the work behind the scenes and never complained about it once."

Which brings us to the man I want you to meet.

## Eleazar: the man who stood when the army left

> "And after him was Eleazar the son of Dodo the Ahohite, one of the three mighty men with David, when they defied the Philistines that were there gathered together to battle, and the men of Israel were gone away: He arose, and smote the Philistines until his hand was weary, and his hand clave unto the sword: and the LORD wrought a great victory that day; and the people returned after him only to spoil." (2 Samuel 23:9-10)

That is the whole account. Two verses. You will not find him anywhere else.

The battle line forms. The Philistines gather. And Israel — the army, the crowd, the men who were supposed to hold that ground — *were gone away*. They left. Every one of them.

Eleazar stayed.

He fought until his hand gave out. He fought until his fingers locked around the hilt and would not open. And the LORD wrought a great victory that day — through one man who did not leave.

There are three things about him I want you to see, because all three of them belong to you.

## 1. Your King chooses your cause

Go back to the day David faced Goliath. His own brothers are sneering at him, and he answers with a question that has echoed for three thousand years:

> "And David said, What have I now done? Is there not a cause?" (1 Samuel 17:29)

**David established the cause. Eleazar chose the king.** That is the order, and it never changes.

Eleazar did not sit down and draft a mission statement. He did not survey the wickedness of his generation and select a hill to die on. He chose his king, and his king decided his cause. His king gave him his direction and his ground.

Now tell me that is not exactly what has happened to you.

The Lord Jesus Christ is King of kings and Lord of lords. You chose Him. You submitted yourself as a subject of that King. Which means the cause is not yours to pick. Whatever the cause is, **He** decides it — not you, not me, not the loudest voice in the room. We say, "Well, I think we ought to do this." Wait a minute. What does the Lord think? What is His direction?

I tell people this all the time: when a church matures, God gives that church a vision of something it ought to do. But He does not hand it out to everybody at once. He gives it to the pastor, the pastor conveys it to the people, and the people are to take it up and carry it. Once he has conveyed it, **it is yours now**. Which means whatever needs doing, you are going to do. Whatever needs paying for, you are going to pay for.

That is not complicated. It is just costly.

## He came out of a cave, and so did you

We are not told exactly when Eleazar joined David, but I have an idea. Look at where David's army came from:

> "David therefore departed thence, and escaped to the cave Adullam: and when his brethren and all his father's house heard it, they went down thither to him. And every one that was in distress, and every one that was in debt, and every one that was discontented, gathered themselves unto him; and he became a captain over them: and there were with him about four hundred men." (1 Samuel 22:1-2)

Even the Bible alliterates: **distress, debt, discontent.** Four hundred men at the bottom of everything, gathering to a man who was not yet king over anybody.

Stop and look at that, because you did the same thing. You took Christ as your King before He has taken His throne over this world.

And notice who David took in. Not the top of society. Not the elite. He took the ruined, the broke, and the bitter — and out of the worst material available, God by His grace made the best of things. These outcasts became the heroes of the nation. That is not what they made of themselves. That is what their king made of them.

It has always worked this way. Look at the men Christ chose:

> "Now when they saw the boldness of Peter and John, and perceived that they were unlearned and ignorant men, they marvelled; and they took knowledge of them, that they had been with Jesus." (Acts 4:13)

Fishermen. A tax collector, who to a Jewish mind was the lowest of the low. Unlearned and ignorant men — and then the world's verdict on them a few chapters later:

> "These that have turned the world upside down are come hither also." (Acts 17:6)

How? Because of their King. That is the whole explanation. When God takes a good-for-nothing and saves him and changes him, the world stands back and says, "What about that?" They cannot account for it. They cannot get it. That is the effect of the King.

## 2. He made his choice, and other people's failures did not make it for him

Here is what Eleazar did **not** do. He did not spend one minute upbraiding the men who ran.

I have been a preacher for forty-eight years and in the ministry for forty-six. In that time I have heard about a lot of men who quit. Men who let sin into their lives, men who walked away, men whose names you would know. I have heard of two or three more just recently. It is disappointing every single time.

But it does not decide anything for me.

Because other people quit does not mean I am going to quit.

Eleazar had every earthly right to run — he was the only one left. Instead he practiced a New Testament principle centuries before there was a New Testament to read it in:

> "Wherefore take unto you the whole armour of God, that ye may be able to withstand in the evil day, and having done all, to stand." (Ephesians 6:13)

The battle starts raging, all Israel runs, and this man says, "No. This is where I belong. This is what I am doing. I am not moving."

So often we let what somebody else does decide what we are going to do. So-and-so is gone. This one quit. That one left. And we discourage ourselves right off the field. But Jesus told us plainly it was going to be that way — so when it **is** that way, what difference does that make to me? I will take my place, and if the enemy comes and I am the only one standing there, then I will fight until I die.

And notice: he didn't die. **Great exploits are never accomplished by people who don't stand.** All those stories you love, all those things you can talk about — none of them happen unless somebody plants his feet and says, "This is my ground."

## 3. His sword became part of him

"His hand clave unto the sword." What does that actually mean?

In the days when British officers led with the sword, a battle might last five, six, ten hours of continuous fighting. When it was finally over, some of those men could not release the weapon. The muscles and tendons of the hand had contracted so violently around the hilt that it could not be pried loose. They had to bring in a surgeon to cut the hand open and peel it back to remove the sword.

That is what happened to Eleazar. He fought so long and so hard that the sword had become part of him.

Now — you already know what our sword is:

> "And take the helmet of salvation, and the sword of the Spirit, which is the word of God:" (Ephesians 6:17)

We ought to be so attached to that Book that somebody would have to pry it out of us. But there is a second thing in this text that we skip past: **the man was proficient.** He didn't just hold the sword — he defeated an army with it. He knew how to use what was in his hand.

> "Study to shew thyself approved unto God, a workman that needeth not to be ashamed, rightly dividing the word of truth." (2 Timothy 2:15)

Too many church members outsource this to the preacher. "I believe what my pastor believes." What is that? "Well, I don't know exactly, but I believe it anyway."

That is precisely how churches end up in a mess. That preacher dies or moves, another man comes in believing something different, and you have not got enough Bible in you to know that what he is preaching is wrong. So now you believe him too.

**You need to know what you believe.** You need to be proficient with your sword — able to handle the Scriptures, able to use them, and able to stand alone with them at work or anywhere else.

I worked the assembly line at Ford. There were plenty of men there who professed to be Christians until you listened to their language for five minutes. I did not argue opinions with them; I used verses. *Here is what the Bible says.* And when the lunch whistle blew, I ate alone — right up until somebody had a real problem. Then they came and sat down beside me and asked, "What does the Bible say about this?"

I had trained myself to be proficient in the Word of God so that I could answer them from the Book instead of from my feelings.

## Not every battle is your battle

Here is where this cuts against the grain of our moment.

Your King owns you. We do not talk this way anymore, but it is Scripture: we are bondservants, which is simply another word for slaves. If He saved your soul, that is what you became. Nobody is asking whether you like it. He is a good Master — loving, longsuffering, merciful, gracious, all of it — but He **is** the Master. And He determines the war.

Which means I do not get to go out and fight everything else.

I will be honest with you: I am tired of political Christianity. Years ago it was the Moral Majority — the movement that put Ronald Reagan in office, and he was a good president. I was just a church member then, back in the late seventies, and my pastor got us involved because we were genuinely concerned about the direction of this country. Then we looked around and found ourselves linked arm-in-arm with Mormons and Catholics and everybody else. My pastor said, "Whoa, whoa, whoa." And we left it.

Now there is another movement, and another after that. Do I care about this country? I do. Do I care who holds office? Very much. But I am not going to attend a political rally and call it Christianity, and I am not going to put my name behind a man whose morality is not merely in question but plainly settled.

We Baptists are different people, and everybody knows it. We are the kind who actually believe the Bible and believe you ought to practice it. Others say, "Well, we believe the Bible, but you have to interpret it" — meaning bend it to fit what they already intend to do. We let the Bible interpret itself. If the Bible says it, that is that. And coalitions do not want people like that around, because people like that get in the way of doing what they wanted to do anyway.

I already have a King, and He has chosen my cause. Do you know what my cause is? The spread of the gospel and the work of the New Testament church. Not because I picked it — because **He** picked it. So that is where I am going to stand.

> "For though we walk in the flesh, we do not war after the flesh: (For the weapons of our warfare are not carnal, but mighty through God to the pulling down of strong holds;)" (2 Corinthians 10:3-4)

Our battle is not to go out and destroy the people we disagree with. Our battle is with our own flesh and with the prince of the power of the air (Ephesians 2:2). That is the warfare we were actually assigned.

And here is what happened to Eleazar when he fought the battle his king gave him: **he won.**

> "But thanks be to God, which giveth us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ." (1 Corinthians 15:57)

I do not know how all the rest of it works out. I know this: we win. The final score is already decided. I do not have to lie awake worrying about overtime.

## He was too exhausted to collect the spoil

Look at that last line one more time: "and the people returned after him only to spoil."

The army that ran came back — to gather the plunder. Eleazar got none of it. He couldn't. His hand was locked shut around a sword he could not release. His exhaustion was so complete that the very thing that proved his faithfulness disqualified him from the reward.

That is all right. **Our King will not forget us.**

> "For God is not unrighteous to forget your work and labour of love, which ye have shewed toward his name, in that ye have ministered to the saints, and do minister." (Hebrews 6:10)

Because there is a day coming when we all stand before Him, and everything gets sorted:

> "Now if any man build upon this foundation gold, silver, precious stones, wood, hay, stubble; Every man's work shall be made manifest: for the day shall declare it, because it shall be revealed by fire; and the fire shall try every man's work of what sort it is." (1 Corinthians 3:12-13)

Some of it burns. And the Lord will not be rushed through the rest of it — He has all eternity, and He can take as long as He likes. There is going to be a group of people nobody ever knew about, and He is going to say, "Let Me tell you their story. Let Me tell you what they did. Let Me tell you what it cost them."

Some of those people are sitting in your church right now. They sacrificed their time and their effort. They gave up the leadership they could have had somewhere else and the things they could have accomplished for themselves — and they handed it to their King instead.

> "...judge nothing before the time, until the Lord come, who both will bring to light the hidden things of darkness, and will make manifest the counsels of the hearts: and then shall every man have praise of God." (1 Corinthians 4:5)

## One name is missing from the list

Read all thirty names in 2 Samuel 23 and then notice who is not there.

Joab. David's own nephew. The commander-in-chief of the entire army. His brothers made the list. Joab did not.

And it was no oversight. Listen to David's dying charge concerning him:

> "Do therefore according to thy wisdom, and let not his hoar head go down to the grave in peace." (1 Kings 2:6)

Joab had been such a problem for so many years that David's final instruction was that he not be allowed to die peacefully — and Solomon carried it out.

David left that name out on purpose.

There are people in churches of whom the same thing will be true. Not because they lacked ability or opportunity — Joab had more of both than anyone on that list — but because of what they were: the problem, the troublemaker, the complainer, the faultfinder, the coward who would not stand.

Do not let that be your name.

## So what will your King say about you?

Be the one who stands his ground. Be the one who hangs on to his sword. Be the one who fights the battle the King chose for him, and leaves the battles He did not choose to somebody else.

Eleazar stood his ground. He made his choice. And his King decided his cause. That is the pattern, and it is available to every one of us.

You may be worn out at the end of the week with nobody patting you on the back. Nobody applauds the work done behind the scenes. That is fine.

**The King sees.**

> "Therefore, my beloved brethren, be ye stedfast, unmoveable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, forasmuch as ye know that your labour is not in vain in the Lord." (1 Corinthians 15:58)

Will you be one of the mighty men? Will you be named at the end? Will your King speak well of you?
